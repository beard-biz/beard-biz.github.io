00 c. 400 BCE Leucippus & Democritus · Abdera, Thrace

Cut it in half. Then cut that half.

Democritus ran no experiment. He ran an argument. Either matter divides without end, or the dividing stops somewhere. He took the second option and called the stopping point átomos, uncuttable. The next atomic theory built on measurement came from Dalton in 1808, about 2,200 years later.

Main characteristics

  • Matter is built from particles that cannot be divided or destroyed.
  • Between the particles is void, containing nothing. Democritus argued motion needs it, since a universe packed solid has nowhere to move into.
  • Atoms differ in shape, size and arrangement, and that is the whole of the explanation for why substances differ. Democritus assigned sharp atoms to bitter tastes and smooth ones to sweet.
  • Atoms last indefinitely. Objects appear and disappear by rearrangement of atoms, never by creation or destruction of them.

Why it stalled

No evidence, and no instrument capable of producing any. Aristotle's rival account, matter as a continuous blend of four elements, matched everyday observation more closely and displaced it. Nothing testable replaced Aristotle until Dalton, in 1808.

01 1803 to 1808 John Dalton · Manchester

The first atom defined by its mass.

Dalton reached atoms from a question about why different gases dissolve in water at different rates. He worked from mass measurements and nothing else. The rule he found: when two elements form more than one compound, the mass of one element per fixed mass of the other lands on small whole-number ratios. Burn carbon two ways and the oxygen figures come out 1.332 g and 2.664 g per gram of carbon.

Main characteristics

  • Every element is built from indivisible particles called atoms.
  • All atoms of one element share the same mass and properties. Atoms of different elements differ, and mass is the property that separates them.
  • Compounds form when atoms combine in fixed, simple whole-number ratios.
  • A chemical reaction rearranges atoms. It does not create them, destroy them, or turn one element into another.
  • The picture that goes with it is a hard sphere with no surface features and no interior, usually called the billiard-ball model. Dalton presented the theory to the Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society in 1803 and published it in A New System of Chemical Philosophy in 1808.

What broke it

Postulate 1 failed in 1897, when Thomson pulled a particle out of the atom. Postulate 2 failed in 1913, when Soddy showed that atoms of one element can carry different masses. He named them isotopes and received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work.

02 1897 to 1904 J. J. Thomson · Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge

Thomson pulled a particle out of the atom.

Hertz had tried this in 1883 and got nothing: charged plates would not bend a cathode ray at all, so he concluded the rays carry no charge. Thomson reproduced the failure, worked out that the leftover gas in the tube was the problem, pumped harder, and the deflection appeared. From the amount of bending he calculated the ratio of the particle's charge to its mass. In his Nobel lecture he put that ratio at 1.7 × 10⁷ in c.g.s. magnetic units, which converts to 1.7 × 10¹¹ C/kg.

Main characteristics

  • The atom contains negatively charged particles. Thomson called them corpuscles; the name electron won out.
  • Their charge-to-mass ratio is 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg. Thomson noted it was 1,700 times the value for a hydrogen ion in electrolysis, which put the particle far below the lightest atom in mass. The modern figure is 1,836.
  • The ratio came out the same for air, hydrogen and carbonic acid, and for every cathode metal he tried. Thomson concluded the particle is a constituent of all matter rather than a property of one substance. This is the measurement that matters, and the answer it gives is that nothing changes.
  • The deflection only appears in a well-pumped tube. Thomson's own explanation: “the absence of deflexion is due to the conductivity conferred on the rarefied gas by the cathode rays.” The rays ionise the residual gas, the gas conducts, and ions reaching the plates cancel the field. At partial vacuum the spot jumps when the battery is connected and then “gradually creeps back to its undeflected position”. That is what Hertz saw.
  • The 1904 model: electrons sit inside a sphere of spread-out positive charge, arranged in rings, giving a neutral atom overall.
  • Thomson never wrote “plum pudding”. An unnamed reporter in The Chemist and Druggist made the raisin comparison in August 1906. Thomson described the positive charge as closer to a fluid than a solid.

What broke it

Positive charge spread across the whole atom produces a weak field everywhere and a strong field nowhere, so it can push a fast alpha particle through a fraction of a degree at most. Geiger and Marsden measured alphas coming back past 90 degrees. The model also accounts for no emission spectrum and no chemical valency.

03 1909 to 1911 Rutherford, with Hans Geiger & Ernest Marsden · Manchester

One alpha in 8,000 comes back.

Geiger and Marsden sat in a blacked-out room for half an hour to let their eyes adjust, then counted flashes on a zinc sulfide screen one at a time, by eye, for months. Rutherford had asked them to check the backward direction as a formality. Thomson's atom predicts nothing arriving there.

Main characteristics

  • Nearly all the mass and all the positive charge sit in a central nucleus.
  • Electrons occupy the volume around it. At one ten-thousandth of the diameter, the nucleus fills about one part in 10¹² of the atom by volume; the rest is empty.
  • Scale: an atom measures about 10⁻¹⁰ m across, the nucleus about 10⁻¹⁴ to 10⁻¹⁵ m, roughly 1/10,000 of the diameter. Blow the atom up to 1 m and the nucleus is a grain of salt at the centre.
  • Results: most alphas passed straight through, about 1 in 8,000 deflected through a large angle, and a few returned along close to their incoming path.
  • Rutherford identified the positive nuclear particle in later work and named it the proton in 1920.

What broke it

A charge moving in a circle is accelerating, and classical electrodynamics says an accelerating charge radiates. A Rutherford electron should lose energy continuously and reach the nucleus in about 10⁻¹¹ seconds, emitting a continuous spread of wavelengths on the way down. Atoms last longer than that, and hydrogen emits four sharp lines in the visible range rather than a continuous spread.

04 1913 Niels Bohr · Copenhagen

Hydrogen emits four lines in the visible range.

Pass the light from hot hydrogen through a prism and four sharp lines appear on black, at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm, in those same positions every time. Bohr stopped asking what the electron does between measurements and instead forbade it any energy outside a fixed list of values. The list reproduces all four wavelengths.

The electron starts in the ground state, n = 1. Raise the slider and it absorbs a photon; lower it and it emits one, which flies to the plate. Four of the possible drops give visible light. Find all four.

Main characteristics

  • Electrons occupy only certain allowed orbits, the stationary states, and radiate nothing while they stay in one. Bohr supplied no reason for this; he asserted it because it stops the spiral.
  • Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh / 2π, with n = 1, 2, 3…
  • Each level has a fixed energy. For hydrogen, Eₙ = −13.6 / n² eV.
  • An electron changes level by absorbing or emitting one photon whose energy matches the gap: ΔE = hf. Fixed gaps give fixed wavelengths, which is what a line spectrum is.
  • The model reproduces hydrogen's visible Balmer series: 656 nm (3→2), 486 nm (4→2), 434 nm (5→2), 410 nm (6→2). It also yields the Rydberg constant, 1.09737 × 10⁷ m⁻¹, from h, c, m and e alone.
  • The instrument here computes straight from Eₙ = −13.606/n² and reads 656.11 nm, while a spectrometer reads 656.28 nm. Two corrections close that gap. Allowing for the nucleus having finite mass rather than being infinitely heavy moves it to 656.47 nm, and light travelling slightly slower in air than in vacuum brings it to 656.29 nm. Bohr's bare formula is right to 0.03%.

What broke it

The model handles one electron and no more: H, He⁺, Li²⁺. Neutral helium has two electrons and Bohr's equations give the wrong spectrum for it. The model also produces no line intensities, no fine structure and no account of chemical bonding, and it supplies no reason why the orbits are quantised.

05 1916 Arnold Sommerfeld · Munich

Bohr's single line is a doublet.

Higher-resolution spectroscopes showed each of Bohr's sharp lines to be a tight cluster. The n=2 levels of hydrogen are separated by about 10.9 GHz, which at 656.28 nm works out to 0.016 nm between components. Sommerfeld's fix: allow elliptical orbits alongside circular ones, and apply special relativity, since an electron swinging close to the nucleus moves fast enough for the correction to register.

Main characteristics

  • Orbits may be elliptical as well as circular, and may be tilted in space.
  • A second quantum number fixes the shape: the azimuthal one, k, written l today. Setting k = n returns Bohr's circle and smaller k narrows the ellipse. The semi-axes are a = n²a₀ and b = nk a₀.
  • A third, magnetic quantum number fixes the orbit's orientation in space, which is what the splitting of lines in a magnetic field, the Zeeman effect, requires.
  • Under special relativity, an electron on an ellipse speeds up near the nucleus and its energy shifts: E(n,k) = −(Rhc/n²)[1 + (α²/n²)(n/k − 3/4)]. Orbits sharing n but differing in k therefore sit at different energies. For n = 3 they fall 2.24, 6.71 and 20.1 µeV below the plain Bohr value, so the k=3 to k=2 gap is a third of the k=2 to k=1 gap. That is the observed fine structure.

What broke it

The model is classical orbits with quantum rules added by hand, and it still gives the wrong spectra for atoms with more than one electron. Heisenberg's 1927 uncertainty principle then removed its foundation: an electron whose position and momentum cannot both be fixed has no trajectory, so there is no ellipse for the model to describe.

06 1924 to 1927 de Broglie · Heisenberg · Schrödinger · Born

Orbits are replaced by probability maps.

De Broglie gave the electron a wavelength in 1924. Heisenberg showed in 1927 that its position and momentum cannot both be fixed, which leaves an orbit with nothing to refer to. Schrödinger wrote the wave equation in 1926 and Born, the same year, identified what the wave measures: the probability of finding the electron at a point.

Each measurement finds the electron in exactly one place, and the place is different every time. One dot tells you nothing. The pattern that builds over hundreds of measurements is the result.

Main characteristics

  • de Broglie, 1924. Matter carries wave properties, λ = h / p. An allowed orbit is one whose circumference fits a whole number of electron wavelengths, which is where Bohr's quantisation rule comes from.
  • Heisenberg, 1927. The uncertainty principle: position and momentum cannot both be known exactly. Nothing in the theory answers the question an orbit would answer.
  • Schrödinger, 1926. A wave equation whose solutions ψ describe the electron as a standing wave around the nucleus. The allowed energies drop out of the boundary conditions instead of being assumed.
  • Born, 1926. |ψ|² is a probability density. Orbits become orbitals, regions of space conventionally drawn to enclose about 90% of the probability.
  • Four quantum numbers describe any electron: n for level and size, l for shape (s, p, d, f), mₗ for orientation, mₛ for spin, which Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit proposed in 1925. Pauli's exclusion principle, also 1925: no two electrons in one atom share all four.

Still standing

A century on, no measurement has contradicted it. It gives the spectra of atoms with many electrons, the s, p, d and f block structure of the periodic table, and the geometry of chemical bonds. This is the model in your textbook.

Afterward · what happened to the nucleus

Then they cut the uncuttable.

1932 · James Chadwick

The neutron

A neutral particle in the nucleus carrying roughly a proton's mass. It accounts for an atom weighing about twice what its charge alone suggests, and for isotopes: same element, same proton count, different neutron count. That finishes off Dalton's second postulate. Chadwick took the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

1964 · Gell-Mann & Zweig

Quarks

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental either. Each is three quarks held by the strong force. The Greek word turned out to be wrong twice over: first when Thomson split off the electron, again when the nucleons came apart. No experiment so far has found structure inside a quark.

1803 to 1926

How the models were replaced

Every replacement above followed a measurement, not a debate. A whole-number mass ratio ended Democritus. A constant e/m across four gases ended Dalton. An alpha returning past 90 degrees ended Thomson. Four fixed wavelengths ended Rutherford. Each time, the number came first and the picture was rebuilt around it.
